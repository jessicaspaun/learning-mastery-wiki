# Learning techniques

[Wiki home](README.md) · [Design practice](practice-design.md) · [Evidence and sources](evidence.md)

## Retrieve from memory

**Use for** facts, explanations, and procedures you need to recall. Read or observe enough to form an initial answer. Close the source, recall or reconstruct the answer, check it, correct errors, then attempt it again later. Useful formats include flashcards, a blank-page reconstruction, explaining a concept aloud, tracing familiar code on paper, or recalling the structure of a song before playing. Match the question to what you want to remember and add performance tasks when the goal includes applying it.

Practice testing is well supported for retention compared with restudy. Benefits for transfer vary with the items, practice format, and target. When recall repeatedly fails, narrow the prompt or give a cue, then remove the cue once the learner can succeed. [Dunlosky et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/) · [Agarwal et al., classroom review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9) · [Pan and Rickard, transfer](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PR_2018.pdf)

## Space practice and relearn after gaps

**Use for** material and skills you want to retain beyond this session. As a starting schedule, learn today, recall or perform tomorrow, revisit several days later, then again the following week. Shorten the next gap for persistent errors and lengthen it when retrieval is reliably successful. Alternate a cold attempt with correction and another opportunity on a later day. If you only have one day, leave room for separate attempts when feasible.

The spacing evidence is especially well established for verbal learning. The useful gap depends on the task and on how long you want to remember it. A meta-analysis supports spacing retrieval episodes and found no reliable overall advantage for expanding over uniform intervals. For physical or musical practice, test whether the chosen schedule improves the performance you care about. [Cepeda et al., spacing experiment](https://laplab.ucsd.edu/articles/Cepeda%20et%20al%202008_psychsci.pdf) · [Latimier et al., spaced retrieval](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8) · [Rawson and Dunlosky, durable retrieval schedules](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21707204/)

## Study worked examples and fade support

**Use for** a procedure, genre, or movement the learner has trouble starting. Show a good example in manageable pieces and point out the decisions that matter. Ask the learner to finish a step, reconstruct a part, or modify the example. Fade the help and test a parallel task. As competency grows, move more time to independent performance and novel tasks.

Worked examples are well supported in mathematics and have domain-specific implementations in programming and writing. Study the rationale when it helps solve the task. The effect of adding explanation prompts depends on the prompts, task, and what the learner already knows. [Barbieri et al., math worked examples](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1) · [Renkl and Atkinson, fading steps](https://doi.org/10.1023/B%3ATRUC.0000021815.74806.f6) · [Bisra et al., self-explanation](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)

## Explain, compare, and generate

**Use for** concepts and situations where you want to reason about why a solution works. Ask “What makes this step valid?”, “When would it fail?”, or “Which of these cases shares the same structure?” Predict an outcome, check it, then revise your explanation. Make an example and a counterexample. Verify explanations against a reference or a test that could reveal a mistake.

For a learner with relevant prerequisites, a brief, designed problem-solving attempt can prepare them to understand subsequent instruction. Productive-failure studies examine the combination of an initial generation task and follow-up teaching. If a learner has no workable starting point, add a model, scaffold, or narrower task. [Bisra et al., self-explanation](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x) · [Sinha and Kapur, problem solving before instruction](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105)

## Interleave confusable tasks

**Use for** choosing among approaches, such as recognizing which math method fits an unlabeled problem. Mix two or three practiced types, hide the category labels, choose a method, and say what cue made it appropriate. If a learner is still acquiring one of the methods, start with several focused repetitions before mixing it in.

The benefit varies by material and comparison group. A meta-analysis found useful effects for some discrimination tasks and for math, mixed results for text, and an advantage for blocked practice in its word studies. Soccer drills and musical passages add motor demands. Use delayed and realistic checks to determine whether switching helps in those settings. [Brunmair and Richter, interleaving](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/) · [Rohrer et al., classroom math experiment](https://uweb.cas.usf.edu/~drohrer/pdfs/Rohrer_et_al_2020JEdPsych.pdf) · [Czyż et al., motor transfer](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1377122)

## Get feedback you can act on

**Use when** you can observe a gap between a goal and an attempt. Describe what happened, identify one improvement, and make another attempt. An answer key can verify recall, tests can reveal code behavior, recordings can expose a timing issue, and readers or coaches can report effects you missed. Ask the learner what they noticed before giving feedback when that serves the task. Get feedback soon enough to use it and periodically test without the cue.

Feedback effects vary widely. Educational research supports informative feedback over simple reinforcement on average. Motor research leaves the optimal timing and frequency uncertain. An illustrative guitar cue is “The pulse slowed at the chord change. Try just those two bars at a speed where the beat stays steady.” Record again to check whether the change helped. [Wisniewski et al., educational feedback](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087) · [McKay et al., motor feedback frequency](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2022.102165)

## Practice a part, then reconnect it to the whole

**Use when** a specific element disrupts performance. Slow or simplify that element, keep one important constraint, fix the error, and put it back into the full task. Practice a chord change within a phrase, a first touch followed by a pass, or a paragraph transition within a whole draft. Restore relevant speed, context, or choice as performance stabilizes. Test under conditions resembling your goal.

When a movement is difficult, try a cue about its effect, such as directing a first touch into space. External-focus cues often helped across the motor tasks in one meta-analysis. Newer analyses raise uncertainty about the size and generality of the advantage. Use a cue the learner can apply and assess the result. [Duke et al., music practice and retention](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429408328851) · [Bergmann et al., soccer skill acquisition](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.772201) · [Chua et al., attentional focus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34843301/) · [Evidence notes on motor learning](evidence.md#limitations-by-technique)

## Match supporting tools to their role

| Tool | Useful role | Follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rereading and videos | First exposure, looking up a gap, and seeing a demonstration | Close the source and recall, predict, explain, or perform |
| Highlighting and notes | Marking evidence or recording a useful idea for later | Turn selected material into questions, examples, or decisions |
| Flashcards and mnemonics | Cueing arbitrary associations, vocabulary, and facts | Use the recalled material in a full task when application matters |
| Diagrams and concept maps | Exploring spatial or causal relationships | Recreate or explain the relationships from memory and verify them |
| Coaching and peer work | Making reasoning visible and getting specific feedback | Have each learner attempt a comparable task independently |
| AI assistance | Offering a hint, checking reasoning, or generating a new example | Preserve the learner’s attempt and include a later independent check |

Tools work best when the activity they enable matches the learning goal. The comparative evidence is strongest for some uses, such as recall practice for retention. See the separate guidance on [AI coaching](codex-coach.md#when-codex-is-the-practice-partner). [Dunlosky et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/) · [Karpicke and Blunt, retrieval and concept mapping](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21252317/) · [Bastani et al., AI in mathematics](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)
