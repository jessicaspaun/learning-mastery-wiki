# Measuring progress and keeping a skill

[Mastery research](index.md) · [Wiki home](../README.md) · [Basic assessment template](../assessment.md)

## Decide what counts as convincing performance

Define the task family, the conditions, the expected quality, and the evidence you’ll collect. A task family is the group of situations the learner should handle, such as recurring guitar transitions in a set of songs or common data-cleaning requests. Include representative examples and difficult cases that matter. Evidence for mastery can include correctness, repeatability, adaptability, ability to recover from an error, and completion within a relevant time. Give the measures a clear relationship to the outcome. [National Academies, learning and transfer](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/9853/chapter/6) · [Soderstrom and Bjork, learning versus performance](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)

| Question | Measurement choice | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can I execute it? | A count, behavior check, or test suite with a clear expected result | Clean chord changes out of the total at a set tempo |
| Can I choose a method? | Unlabeled cases with different appropriate responses | Mixed math problems that require different setups |
| How good is the output? | A short rubric with examples of what each level looks like | A reader locates a proposal’s decision and can describe its rationale |
| Can I recover? | Include an interruption, error, or changed requirement that fits the task | Diagnose a failing test and show what fixed it |
| Can I handle tradeoffs? | Track two relevant outcomes or set one as a constraint | In soccer, log completion alongside the quality of the pass choice |

## Separate conditions that answer different questions

- **Baseline.** Try familiar and representative items before training. A few samples give useful context about ordinary variability when the time cost fits.
- **Practice check.** Use frequent checks to find the next change. Record prompts, warm-up, time, examples, and tools that helped.
- **Delayed retention.** Repeat a comparable task before target-specific review, after a gap that matters. Use the same standard and equivalent conditions.
- **Transfer.** Use fresh tasks from the same task family, then tasks with a relevant change. Decide ahead of time which resources a person would normally use.
- **Maintenance.** Test again once actual use has become infrequent or before an important performance. Adjust practice frequency from the results.

Repeating a test can itself teach or rehearse the material. Separate reusable benchmark tasks from fresh test variants when familiarity with the item could swamp the skill you are trying to assess. Use examples of comparable difficulty and note any differences you can identify. In an authentic task, separate permitted tools such as documentation or a chord chart from tutoring cues. The meaning of “independent” depends on the goal. [Pan and Rickard, retrieval practice transfer](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PR_2018.pdf) · [Soderstrom and Bjork, learning versus performance](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)

## Start with the smallest useful comparison

**Choose one primary outcome.** For guitar, count how many of twelve transitions land cleanly and in time on each unassisted first take at the same tempo. Also note if the player stops, since a run can meet a transition target and still fail the performance goal.

**Collect repeated observations.** Suppose a learner records 6/12, 7/12, and 6/12 at baseline, then 8/12, 7/12, and 9/12 after three sessions that use a different practice cue. This suggests possible improvement on the sampled phrase. Look at the individual takes. The change could reflect learning across those sessions, a particular cue, added familiarity, or ordinary variability. Test a fresh progression across another few days before broadening the claim.

**Compare two practice methods if the choice matters.** Choose two reasonably matched task sets, such as similar transitions or exercise families. Assign different methods, keep total practice and the eventual test conditions comparable, and use a fresh set to see whether the result repeats. Keep track of prior exposure and whether the method used for one set spills into the other. When learning carries over, the first block changes what the learner brings to the second, so reversing order on the same skill cannot fully reset the comparison. These are practical comparisons. Formal single-case research uses stronger designs, such as multiple baselines and repeated independent demonstrations of a change. [What Works Clearinghouse, single-case designs and standards](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/handbooks/)

**Keep measurement and coaching separate long enough to see independent performance.** During a check, let the learner finish using the agreed support. Then provide feedback and practice the error. If you change the rubric, tempo, tool access, task, or amount of help, log the change. For scoring subjective work, rate the drafts or clips in mixed order when practical and have a second person score a small sample to find ambiguous criteria.

## Maintain rarely used skills

A 2025 meta-analysis of procedural skills with a motor component pooled years of skill retention research. Across the tasks examined, accuracy, speed, and combined measures showed different patterns of decline with nonuse. Conditions such as task and practice history varied. The reported group averages describe loss of initial gains over time. To choose a refresh interval, use the performance you need and a sample from your own task. A review of complex professional competencies found a thinner evidence base, which limits generalization to open-ended activities. [Tatel and Ackerman, procedural skill retention meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40455501/) · [Systematic review of competence retention in complex professions](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1747938X19304026)

1. **Record your last stable baseline.** Keep a sample and enough context to reproduce the standard.
2. **Set the next check before you need the skill.** For a piece you’ll perform in several weeks, choose a check early enough to repair a lapse.
3. **Start by doing the task.** After the usual warm-up or setup, try a representative example before reviewing target details. That attempt shows you what still works.
4. **Refresh whatever degraded.** Rebuild a prerequisite, run a short focused drill, review one reference, or repeat the full workflow when coordination across steps is the issue.
5. **Retest on another day.** Shorten the next gap if you needed major supports, and extend it if the skill remains dependable. For activities with established qualification standards, use the prescribed assessments and refresh requirements.

For rarely used knowledge, revisit key distinctions through spaced retrieval. For rarely used procedures, check both factual recall and execution. For rarely used team skills, assess whether communication and coordination hold when the group changes. A team’s performance depends on how individuals work together. Evidence for team-level decay is thinner than evidence for individual tasks. [Latimier et al., spaced retrieval meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8) · [Arthur et al., empirical paper on team skill decay](https://doi.org/10.1080/08959285.2024.2436171)

## Restart after a long break

Choose a small task from the level you last performed confidently. Note which elements return after a general warm-up, which require a reminder, and which need rebuilding. Practice at a level that gives you enough successful attempts to see what improves. Then bring back one real condition, such as tempo, audience, time limit, unfamiliar inputs, or an opponent. This sequence is a practical application of the evidence on retention and transfer. It is especially useful when the break and the next task differ from those in the research.
