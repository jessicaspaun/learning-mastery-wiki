# Practice design, feedback, and plateaus

## Diagnose the plateau before changing the plan

A plateau is an observation about a measure over a period of time. Compare several attempts at similar difficulty, with the same scoring and access to help. Record both speed and accuracy when they trade off. If practice tasks have become harder, include a recurring benchmark alongside them. Individual response-time curves can look different from averaged curves, and controlled experiments found that tracking which strategy people used explained improvements better than pooling attempts with different strategies. The studies address changes in response time on relatively constrained tasks. For a complex skill, inspect the attempts behind a score to see which components are changing. [Heathcote et al., individual learning curves](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03212979) · [Delaney et al., strategy-specific improvement](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00001)

1. **Locate the first repeatable breakdown.** Compare an attempt that worked with one that failed. Write what you can see or verify. “First touch went toward the defender” is observable. “I scanned too late” is a hypothesis to test.
2. **Identify what changes the outcome.** Reduce one demand, add one useful cue, or compare two examples that differ in one meaningful feature. If the same error occurs even with time and help, check the prerequisite or the action itself. If it appears only in context, examine a transition, a decision, or an attention demand.
3. **Practice the smallest version that preserves the problem.** Keep the beat across a guitar transition, the claim when rewriting its supporting paragraph, or a moving opponent in a soccer decision drill.
4. **Run a fresh check.** Test one comparable attempt after a gap and one new but relevant case. If the drill improved and the full task did not, examine what information or judgment the drill left out. [Soderstrom and Bjork, learning versus performance](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000) · [National Research Council, learning and transfer](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/9853/chapter/6)

| Plateau you observe | A useful contrast | Practice if the contrast isolates the problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Guitar.** Both chords sound clean alone and the transition falls behind the beat | Play with the same pulse from the beat before the switch through the beat after it | Loop that span, then start one bar earlier and continue one bar later |
| **Writing.** Readers identify the claim and disagree about which evidence supports it | Ask them to map a paragraph to the claim, then compare two paragraph orders | Write the relationship explicitly, then test a revised passage with a fresh reader |
| **Math.** Correct on worksheets and stuck on unlabeled tasks | Give paired problems that look alike and require different methods | Have the learner choose a method, state the deciding feature, and solve |
| **Programming.** Can edit a tutorial project and stalls on a fresh brief | Give a small specification with one example input and output. Ask for a plan before code. | Practice choosing a representation and spelling out edge cases, then implement the plan |
| **Soccer.** First touch is clean in a wall drill and loses possession in games | Keep the pass similar and move a defender between two available outlets | Practice scanning, choosing the side, and receiving in one activity |

These are diagnostic examples. When the observed result could have several causes, gather another attempt or ask someone qualified to inspect the work.

## Choose what to isolate

Part practice lets a learner repeat an error-prone action with fewer competing demands. Whole practice preserves the timing and relationships among components. Consider **complexity**, or how many parts must be coordinated, and **organization**, or how much each part changes in response to the others. The more closely the parts interact, the more useful it is to keep those interactions in a simplified version of the whole activity. A motor-learning meta-analysis generally supported these distinctions, with sparse evidence for several comparisons. A separate juggling study found similar average retention and transfer after part and whole regimens, with results varying by grade. [Fontana et al., part and whole practice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20038005/) · [Chan et al., juggling study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26112404/)

Practice through a boundary when the boundary is where errors arise. Two paragraphs can each read well and still make a poor transition. An isolated first touch can be technically sound and leave the player unable to use a passing lane. Overlap adjacent pieces or simplify a whole task while keeping the relevant relationship. Use whole-task attempts to see which component to isolate next. These applications follow from the part–whole distinction and the need to preserve the demands of the target. [Fontana et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20038005/) · [Bergmann et al., soccer practice design](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.772201)

## Make feedback useful for the next attempt

| Feedback type | What it tells you | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outcome | How the result differs from the target | “The note arrived after the beat,” or “The test expected one record and got two” |
| Process | Where the attempt diverged and how | “You moved to the new chord after the downbeat,” or “The loop appended the same record twice” |
| Strategy | What to try or investigate next | “Record the two-beat switch at a slower pulse,” or “Trace the list after each append” |

The learner may already have **intrinsic feedback** from the sound, movement, or visible result. A recording, test, reader, or coach can add information to that signal. Give a specific observation, ask the learner to explain or predict a correction, and let them retry. A large educational synthesis found that feedback with usable information tended to be more effective than basic reinforcement. In two experiments on a movement with angle and timing targets, numerical error or graded feedback supported improvement. Binary feedback produced no detectable improvement from baseline in the tested condition. Different tasks reveal different amounts without added feedback. [Wisniewski et al., educational feedback](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087) · [St Germain et al., motor feedback experiments](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-022-02170-5)

**Timing.** Immediate feedback is convenient when the next attempt depends on the correction. End-of-attempt or end-of-set feedback gives the learner time to complete the performance and compare their own diagnosis with the evidence. A 2026 synthesis of 51 computer-assisted learning studies found an average immediate-versus-delayed effect close to zero, with substantial variation. A separate preregistered experiment across 38 college classes also found an average close to zero for immediate feedback versus a delay of a few days. In controlled experiments on learning from prose, delayed quiz corrections improved later cued recall. The findings cover different tasks and delays. [Kandemir et al., feedback timing meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-026-10117-8) · [Fyfe et al., ManyClasses experiment](https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459211027575) · [Butler et al., quiz-feedback experiments](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18194050/)

**Frequency.** Try feedback when it changes the next decision, when the learner repeatedly misjudges a result, or when a coach can see a pattern across attempts. Include attempts with the feedback conditions that will be available during real performance. A motor-learning synthesis of 61 papers found no reliable advantage of feedback on fewer trials and no reliable change in relative effects from practice to delayed retention. The typical study was small, and effects varied. Choose a frequency that fits the exercise and verify it with delayed performance. [McKay et al., feedback frequency meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2022.102165)

**Learner control.** A learner can choose which attempt to review, and the coach can ask them what they expect to learn from the review. A 2025 meta-analysis found benefits for learner-controlled motor feedback on retention and transfer. Bias-sensitive analyses of earlier self-controlled practice studies estimated much smaller effects, and two experiments found no advantage of choosing when to receive feedback in those tasks. A 2026 putting experiment with 28 participants found a learner-controlled advantage at 24 hours. The scheduling advantage across tasks remains uncertain. Requesting feedback when you have a specific question is a useful workflow to try. [Wang et al., self-controlled feedback meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15091291) · [McKay et al., reporting bias analysis](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2023.2207255) · [St Germain et al., motor experiments](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-022-02170-5) · [2026 putting study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13114186/)

## Vary the cue or the decision you need to learn

**Variability** changes features across attempts, such as the incoming speed of a soccer pass. **Blocked or random order** describes how different practice conditions are scheduled. You can vary conditions within one skill and still return to another target in a separate block. Repetition can make one change easy to inspect. Mixing tasks can make learners distinguish when to select each response. Add variation tied to the performance you need. For math, mix familiar problem types so the learner has to choose an approach. For soccer, vary the defender's position so the learner must scan for an actual opening. [Rohrer et al., classroom math trial](https://uweb.cas.usf.edu/~drohrer/pdfs/Rohrer_et_al_2020JEdPsych.pdf) · [Bergmann et al., soccer skill acquisition](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.772201)

Evidence on random versus blocked motor practice depends on the test and setting. A 2024 meta-analysis found an advantage for random scheduling on transfer in laboratory motor tasks. The estimate in applied tasks was smaller and uncertain. A separate synthesis using change scores found no reliable overall advantage for high interference in sports and physical education. A guitar or soccer learner can use focused repetitions to find a correction, then vary relevant cues to see whether the correction survives. [Czyż et al., motor transfer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11349744/) · [Ammar et al., sport and physical education](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-024-09892-z)

The challenge point framework proposes choosing task difficulty in relation to a learner's current skill and to what information becomes available when they try. A 2025 scoping review described applications across education, motor learning, rehabilitation, and sport, and found gaps in field testing. As a practical check, look for attempts that reveal interpretable errors and leave enough room to act on feedback. If the cause is hard to identify, reduce one demand. If the relevant choice has become predictable, change the cue that determines the response. Treat a fixed success percentage as specific to the exercise and threshold you set. [Thomas et al., challenge point framework scoping review](https://doi.org/10.1080/00222895.2025.2508283) · [Hodges and Lohse, extended framework](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2021.2015917)

## Use observation and mental rehearsal to prepare a real attempt

Watch a model with a question in mind. Pause before the important action, predict what happens, then compare. For a guitarist this might mean hearing the pulse and imagining the change before playing it. For soccer it might mean predicting which lane opens and then trying the decision with a partner. When observing a writing or programming example, explain the author's choice and then make the equivalent choice in a fresh example. The latter examples apply prediction and self-explanation to cognitive tasks. [Bisra et al., self-explanation meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x) · [Sentance et al., prediction and investigation in programming](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/229013/)

A meta-analysis found a small average performance benefit from mental rehearsal of motor skills after adjustment for publication bias. A review of reaching adaptation found benefits from observing with considerably larger effects from physical practice in the studied tasks. A later systematic review of combined observation and imagery found promising results and infrequent retention and transfer testing. Use mental rehearsal to get ready for an attempt and grade the movement or decision in the relevant setting. [Toth et al., mental practice meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2020.101672) · [Rudisch et al., observational practice](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-024-00271-5) · [Binks et al., combined observation and imagery](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2025.2575511)

## Test an alternate strategy when a familiar one gets in the way

When the same method repeatedly produces the same error, ask what clue made it seem appropriate. Compare one case where the method works and one where that clue misleads. In algebra or geometry, check the quantity and its units before applying a familiar proportional relationship. In programming, compare a boundary case with a typical input and trace the condition that handles each. Experiments have documented familiar approaches blocking better ones in chess, and math studies have documented learners applying linear models to nonlinear geometry problems. The usefulness of a particular comparison exercise still depends on the learner and task. [Bilalić et al., chess fixation](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2007.02.001) · [Math study of linear overgeneralization](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7118200/)

For a low-stakes exercise, elicit a prediction, try a case where it fails, inspect the failure, and devise a test that distinguishes the old rule from the new one. Studies of error-management training combined active exploration with direct encouragement to learn from errors and reported benefits on subsequent transfer, particularly to structurally different tasks in that body of research. The feedback and revision steps explain how an error becomes an opportunity to learn. [Keith and Frese, error-management training meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18211135/)

## Keep a short record of what you changed

| Record | One example |
| --- | --- |
| Repeatable breakdown | “On three recordings at this tempo, the downbeat arrives before the new chord.” |
| Explanation to test | “I start moving my fretting hand too late even when I can name the next chord.” |
| Change for the next practice | “Work from the preceding beat at the same slower tempo, using the recording to check the onset.” |
| Comparable delayed test | “Play the same progression before rehearsing it, at the same benchmark tempo.” |
| Relevant variation | “Try the same switch in another song with the same beat pattern.” |
| Next decision | “If the switch is late only in full takes, practice the transition from farther back in the phrase.” |

These records support local decisions. Compare results across a few comparable attempts and log competing explanations, such as changing tempo, feedback, or familiarity with the test.
