# How expertise develops

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Mastery deep dive](index.md) · [Assess progress](../assessment.md)

Expert performers can usually recognize familiar situations quickly, handle routine demands reliably, and notice when a standard approach needs adjustment. Those abilities may develop at different rates. A guitarist can play chord changes fluently and still struggle to follow a singer’s cue. A programmer can write a familiar search routine quickly and still need time to diagnose an unusual failure. Define the kind of performance you want to improve and collect an example that shows how the learner handles it today. [Ericsson, measuring reproducible expert performance](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2007.02946.x) · [Hatano and Inagaki, two courses of expertise](https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/repo/huscap/all/25206/)

## How the performance changes

| What the learner can do now | What to develop next | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recognize the task and follow a model | Understand the steps and identify the most useful cues | A math learner marks the relationship that identifies which operation is valid. |
| Perform with prompts or in a predictable setting | Detect and correct errors with progressively fewer prompts | A writer revises a paragraph using a model, then revises a fresh paragraph using a short criterion. |
| Perform a familiar version independently | Build fluency while preserving accuracy and attention to the surrounding task | A guitarist practices a chord change until they can keep time and listen for the accompaniment. |
| Perform consistently on familiar cases | Recognize exceptions, adapt to meaningful variation, and recover when a strategy fails | A soccer player changes their receiving angle when the defender removes the expected passing lane. |

Use the rows as overlapping descriptions. Fitts and Posner’s classic model described changes from effortful, explicit control toward smoother and more automatic performance. Experimental work in motor learning finds that intentional strategies and adaptation can operate together and on different timescales. A learner may be fluent at one component and still need a worked example for another. Reassess the new component when a task changes. [Fitts and Posner, *Human Performance*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nlmcatalog/136113) · [Taylor and Ivry, strategies in motor learning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4330992/) · [Motor learning on multiple timescales](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4672876/)

## Recognizing structure and building fluency

People with domain expertise can encode recurring configurations as **chunks**, or meaningful groups of elements. Chess experiments found a much larger memory advantage for plausible positions than for shuffled boards. Later studies found a residual advantage even with randomized material from the same domain, where small meaningful configurations can still occur. In physics, Chi and colleagues found that experts tended to group problems by underlying principles, while novices more often grouped them by surface features such as the presence of an inclined plane. These comparisons suggest useful practice targets and leave open how much each training method contributes to developing them. [Chase and Simon, chess perception](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/Chase1973.pdf) · [Sala and Gobet, domain-specific recall meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5323477/) · [Chi et al., physics problem representation](https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/resources/Research/cognitive/Chi1981.pdf)

- **Train recognition with contrasting cases.** Give two tasks that look similar and require different responses. Then give two tasks that look different and share a principle. Have the learner predict, explain the relevant cue, and check the answer.
- **Practice an entire meaningful unit.** A guitar phrase includes timing and the transition into the next phrase. A block of code includes its input, invariant, and effect. Keep those relationships visible while simplifying the unit.
- **Check accuracy at the new speed.** On a familiar task, measure execution and a second demand that actually matters, such as keeping time while listening to another player. Add this demand after basic execution is stable. Test a fresh case as well.

These are practice applications. Experimental math modules reported gains from matching different representations and extracting relevant structure. Automaticity research shows that practice on consistent mappings can make responses faster and less dependent on deliberate processing. Fast judgment deserves confidence when the environment offers reliable cues and the learner has had opportunities to learn from feedback. [Kellman et al., perceptual learning in math](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2009.01053.x) · [Logan, theory and tests of automatization](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.95.4.492) · [Kahneman and Klein, conditions for intuitive expertise](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19739881/)

## Adjust guidance and difficulty separately

**Guidance** is how much information the practice supplies. Start with a model or a meaningful cue when relevant prior knowledge is missing. Fade steps as the learner can supply them. In a 2025 meta-analysis of 60 studies, relatively high instructional support improved outcomes on average for learners with lower prior knowledge (standardized mean difference about 0.51) and reduced them for learners with higher prior knowledge (about −0.43). Effects varied considerably between studies and domains. Use evidence from the learner’s performance on the specific task to decide how much support to provide. [Tetzlaff et al., expertise reversal meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142)

**Task difficulty** is what the learner must manage, such as tempo, number of decisions, ambiguity, or opponent pressure. The challenge point framework proposes that the useful difficulty of a motor task depends on both the task and the performer. A 2025 scoping review found varied applications and limited practical evidence. Change one relevant constraint, inspect the resulting errors, and compare later independent performance. For a math learner who can solve equations with reminders, you can fade the reminders on the same problem type. To add difficulty, introduce a mixed set after they can solve its component types. [Guadagnoli and Lee, challenge point framework](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15130871/) · [Thomas et al., application scoping review](https://doi.org/10.1080/00222895.2025.2508283)

## What deliberate-practice research can tell you

Ericsson and colleagues used *deliberate practice* for effortful activities designed to improve specific aspects of performance within a domain with established methods of training. Ericsson and Harwell later distinguished individually designed, teacher-guided practice from self-directed *purposeful practice*. Both can involve a defined improvement goal, directed trials, informative feedback, and repeated revision. The categories matter when comparing studies. For a solo learner, the useful question is what the next attempt will teach. A guitar learner who consistently misses a chord change can record it, diagnose where timing slips, practice through the transition at a manageable tempo, then retest at the target tempo on another day. [Ericsson et al., original framework](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363) · [Ericsson and Harwell, definitions and reanalysis](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396)

The evidence answers two different questions. Structured practice programs can improve measured skills, as illustrated by comparisons of simulation training packages in health professions. Such packages combine repetition, feedback, clear standards, and differences in instruction. Separately, cross-domain studies ask how well past practice hours account for differences between people. Macnamara and colleagues found differing associations across domains, and Ericsson and Harwell obtained a larger association using a narrower inclusion rule. A later violin study found that accumulated practice distinguished less-accomplished players from more-accomplished ones, while the best and good groups had comparable amounts. Retrospective time estimates, how performers were grouped, and how practice was defined all limit causal interpretation. The research provides no fixed hour quota for an individual’s mastery. [McGaghie et al., simulation review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3102783/) · [Macnamara et al., cross-domain meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24986855/) · [Ericsson and Harwell, reanalysis](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396) · [Macnamara and Maitra, violin replication](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731745/)

Prior experience, access to training and feedback, and individual differences can affect both practice exposure and observed performance. A 2024 twin study found that genetic variation associated with scores on a musical auditory-discrimination test remained across reported levels of practice. The estimate describes variation in that sample, with that test and model. The design leaves the effect of a specific exercise on an individual open. For a learning plan, assess current performance, identify accessible support, and judge the response to training over time. [Mosing et al., twin study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11433073/) · [Hambrick et al., expertise evidence review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7461852/)

## When to specialize and when to explore

A 2025 review of exceptional performers reported that greater early breadth and more gradual progress appeared in several retrospective comparisons of adults at the very highest performance levels. A 2026 analysis used simulations and longitudinal chess and athletics data to show that selecting only eventual top performers can weaken or reverse associations. This leaves uncertainty about whether changing a learner’s early specialization would cause better long-term outcomes. For adult self-study, set a concrete primary goal and use adjacent activities when they train a missing capability. A guitarist learning to accompany a singer can spend some sessions playing with a singer and keep working on guitar technique in the other sessions. [Güllich et al., paths to elite performance](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adt7790) · [Bilalić et al., selection bias in expertise research](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/246306/)

## Train and test adaptive expertise

Routine expertise supports efficient performance when the situation is familiar. Adaptive expertise includes recognizing where the routine applies, noticing a consequential change, and using existing knowledge to learn a response. Concepts, varied problems, feedback, and opportunities to explain choices are promising supports. A 2025 realist review of work-based learning included 10 studies and developed proposals about how workplace conditions support that development. The literature has varied definitions and measures of adaptation. [Hatano and Inagaki, two courses of expertise](https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/repo/huscap/all/25206/) · [Carbonell et al., review of adaptive expertise](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/how-experts-deal-with-novel-situations-a-review-of-adaptive-exper/) · [Groenier et al., realist review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12186-025-09366-5)

**Try three tasks.** First, assess one familiar task. Next, change a condition that makes the usual approach insufficient and ask the learner to identify why. Then supply a useful reference and observe how they use it to update their approach. For programming, use a familiar parser, introduce quoted fields that can contain a separator, and give the learner the format specification. Score standard performance, recognition of the failed assumption, quality of the revision, and ability to explain the change. The third task is an example of assessing *preparation for future learning*. A classroom statistics study found a benefit from student invention followed by instruction when the transfer test itself included an opportunity to learn from a resource. [Schwartz and Martin, statistics experiments](https://aaalab.stanford.edu/assets/papers/2004/Inventing_to_prepare_for_future_learning.pdf)
