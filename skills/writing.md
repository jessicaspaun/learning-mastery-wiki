# Learning to write

## Decide who the text serves

A writing goal needs a reader, genre, and intended effect. A memo might help a manager make a decision. A help page should let a new user finish a task. An argument should make a defensible claim and handle an objection. A fictional scene might make a character’s motivation clear through action and detail. Choose a model in the genre you want to practice.

Use a short baseline draft. Ask one reader what they think the text is trying to do, what they understood, and where their attention or understanding broke. Choose one craft target you can practice in the next draft.

## Study a model, draft, and revise

1. **Read as a writer.** Mark the function of two or three moves in a strong example, such as how the opening gives context or how evidence supports a claim.
2. **Imitate the move with new content.** Draft about 200 words for your intended reader. If the model previews a decision up front, try doing that with your own decision.
3. **Get specific feedback.** Ask your reader to paraphrase the main point before you explain it. Ask which passage gave them enough information and where they needed more.
4. **Revise for a purpose.** Change the order, evidence, explanation, or scene action to address what the reader experienced. Copy-edit once the larger structure is working.
5. **Compare the drafts.** Ask a fresh reader to try the same task if possible. Capture the change that helped so you can apply it to a new piece.

## Pick a feedback question

| Goal | What to ask a reader to do |
| --- | --- |
| Clear argument | State the claim in their own words and point to the strongest support and the largest unresolved question |
| Useful instructions | Follow the instructions with a sample input and mark the first step where they need extra information |
| Clear workplace memo | Identify the decision, who owns it, and what information they would need before deciding |
| Engaging scene | Describe what the character wants and where tension changes, citing a moment in the text |
| Better sentence-level clarity | Mark one sentence they had to reread and explain where they lost the thread |

A reader’s interpretation is an observation. Use it to diagnose the problem, then test a revision. Reading and giving feedback on someone else’s draft is another way to practice identifying craft choices. [Cho and MacArthur, learning by reviewing](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021950)

## Check retention and transfer

The next week, write on a different topic for a similar audience without looking at the first draft. Use the same rubric. For broader transfer, change the reader or the genre and explain which parts of your strategy you adjusted. Save a dated baseline, draft, feedback, and revision so you can see the change in your work.

## What the research covers

A best-evidence reanalysis of secondary-school studies found positive average effects for explicit strategy instruction, process writing, feedback, prewriting, and peer assistance. The U.S. What Works Clearinghouse recommends a model, practice, and reflection cycle in secondary writing. A higher-education synthesis supports formative peer feedback in academic writing while reporting relatively few well-controlled studies. The workplace and fiction exercises above are adaptations for different audiences and genres. [Graham et al., best-evidence meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11145-024-10539-2) · [What Works Clearinghouse writing guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/22) · [Huisman et al., peer feedback](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2018.1545896)
