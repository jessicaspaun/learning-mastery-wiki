## Practice technique and decisions

Pick a behavior that shows up in a game. “Receive the ball under light pressure and play a useful pass within two touches when the pass is available” gives you something to observe. Look separately at the player’s scan, first touch, choice, timing, and pass execution. A successful pass on a predictable drill and a successful pass in a match draw on different information.

## Example progression for the first touch

1. **Warm up and set a baseline.** Take several passes from a partner or rebound wall. Note how often your first touch sets up the next action. Try a few lightly contested possessions to see whether the same problem appears.
2. **Get focused repetitions.** Receive from a comfortable distance and take the first touch toward a gate. Change the incoming angle once you can control the ball reliably. Use the gate as feedback.
3. **Introduce a live decision.** In a small 3v1 keep-away setup, give the receiver two teammates as outlets. The defender can change position and press. The receiver scans, then directs the first touch and chooses a pass based on the available space.
4. **Adjust a constraint.** Give the receiver more time, enlarge the space, or start the defender farther away if the choice is rarely available. Increase pressure gradually by adjusting space, numbers, or the defender’s starting position.
5. **Check against different opponents.** Use a small-sided game or a scrimmage. Count occasions where there was a genuine option. Review a few choices, including sensible ones that were executed poorly.

One feedback cue could be “Can your first touch put the ball in a space where you can play the next pass?” See what changes in the next few attempts. Use a coach, partner, or video to observe choices and execution when available. For a solo session, use a wall and target gates for control and accuracy, then arrange a later partner or game check for live decisions.

## Check retention and transfer

- **Retention.** After warming up on the next practice day, repeat a comparable reception drill before revisiting the cue.
- **Near transfer.** Change the angle, speed, or pressure and see whether the first touch still sets up an option.
- **Game transfer.** In a match or scrimmage, track eligible receiving opportunities, how often the first touch leaves a viable option, and whether the chosen action and execution make sense. Consider position, teammate options, and opponent pressure when comparing sessions.

For anticipation practice, pause a first-person or game clip before the next pass. Name the cue you see and predict one plausible option. Resume the clip and compare. Check whether those observations show up during play.

## What the research covers

A soccer-specific systematic review found support for different practice designs depending on the outcome and promising evidence for representative, exploratory tasks. A small-sided-games meta-analysis in youth team sports reported technical gains and found no eligible studies measuring tactical behavior. More recent expert consensus describes ways to adjust space, player numbers, and rules. The drills above apply those ideas to recreational practice. [Bergmann et al., soccer skill acquisition](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.772201) · [Clemente et al., small-sided games](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.667041) · [Beato et al., design consensus](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/28545/)

Video-based perceptual drills show promise for anticipation and decision measures. The studies measure longer-term learning and on-field transfer less often. A soccer experiment found no game transfer from a generic 3D object-tracking task. Use video as a way to rehearse reading relevant cues, then check live decisions. [Video-based soccer training review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9686440/) · [Soccer 3D tracking transfer study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2024.102770)
